About this document. It is written to be read by an officer triaging a queue, and to be understood by the victim handing it over. This page stands alone: it should be enough to decide whether the case is investigable. That assumption has not yet been validated with practitioners and is being tested.

On 9 August 2026, 0.42100000 BTC was moved out of a wallet belonging to the reporting person without their authorisation. The wallet had been under continuous monitoring since 6 August 2026.

The funds left in a single transaction and were split across three addresses within one minute. The largest portion, 0.31 BTC, was moved again at 16:10 and reached an address that appears to belong to a cryptocurrency exchange.

A smaller portion of 0.03 BTC passed through a service that does not publish its records and could not be followed further. This is stated rather than omitted; the gap is documented on page 3.

Every movement below is recorded on the public Bitcoin blockchain and can be verified independently by any party, using the transaction identifiers on page 2.

Methodology

tracing rule

Funds are traced first-in, first-out. Where stolen and unrelated funds are mixed in one address, the earliest deposit is treated as the earliest withdrawal, following Clayton's Case (1816) as applied to mixed accounts. FIFO is a legal convention, not a statement of physical fact: the blockchain does not record which specific funds moved.

heuristics used

common input ownershipinputs to one transaction share a controller63.46%

These error rates are high, and are stated rather than omitted. The lowest error is achieved by applying both heuristics together rather than either alone; neither is relied on in isolation in this report. Figures are from a 2022 published analysis of blockchain address clustering algorithms; the full citation is held in the project's docs/11-prior-art.md and is to be printed here before release.

No error rate is yet cited for deposit-address clustering. The service attribution on page 3, and the actionability statement on page 1 that rests on it, therefore carry an undisclosed error rate. That is a known gap in this report and is recorded here rather than left for the reader to discover.

what this report does not claim

It does not identify any person. Attribution is to services and address clusters, never to individuals.
It does not establish that a crime occurred. That is a matter for investigation.
It does not prove which specific coins moved. FIFO is an accounting convention applied to mixed funds.
It does not assert anything about funds that entered services which do not publish records.
It does not indicate that funds are recoverable. Most stolen cryptocurrency is not recovered.
It does not claim to be admissible evidence. No heuristic-based address clustering algorithm has been successfully admitted in court proceedings: under the Daubert standard an algorithm should have a known error rate, and none has been able to report one. This report states the error rates it has, which addresses that gap for two of its three heuristics — but stating an error rate does not by itself establish admissibility, and one rate remains uncited.
